## What each ASUS laptop family is actually for

The families are not tiers of quality. They're different bets about what you'll do with the machine.

If you only take one thing from that table: **Vivobook and TUF are where the value is, Zenbook and ROG are where the polish and performance are, and ProArt sits in between for people who earn money from colour accuracy.**

## The model name is not the specification

This is the part that catches buyers out. ASUS reuses names aggressively. "Vivobook 16" covers configurations with wildly different processors, panels, memory and storage. "TUF Gaming A15" likewise.

The two things that identify what you're actually buying:

1. **The full model code** printed in the specifications table or on the box. That code, not the marketing name, tells you the exact configuration the store is selling.
2. **The panel details**, listed as resolution, refresh rate, and typically brightness or colour coverage. A cheap 16-inch laptop with a dim, low-coverage panel is a genuinely worse experience than a 14-inch one with a good screen, and it's usually the first thing cut to hit a price.

Memory deserves its own warning. On thin Zenbook and Vivobook models, RAM is frequently soldered to the board. If you buy 8GB to save money, you will still have 8GB in three years. Where a slot exists, adding memory later is far cheaper than buying a higher trim up front.

On gaming machines, look for the graphics power figure rather than just the GPU name. The same GPU in two different TUF or ROG models can run at very different power limits, and that's the difference between a smooth frame rate and a warm laptop with a fan that never stops.

## Matching the machine to what you actually do

### Students and office work

Priorities are battery life, weight, keyboard, and a screen you can stare at for eight hours. A Zenbook if you carry it around constantly; a mid or upper Vivobook if it mostly stays on a desk. 16GB of memory and a 512GB SSD are the sensible floor now — anything less starts to feel tight the moment you open a browser with thirty tabs, a video call and a spreadsheet.

For accounting-style work and anything with lots of numeric entry, a 15 or 16-inch body with a numeric keypad beats a 14-inch machine. That's a preference, but it's a real one if you spend the day in spreadsheets.

### Creators

Colour coverage matters more than raw speed for photo and video work. ProArt exists for this, and the upper Vivobook OLED models are worth comparing against it if your editing load is moderate. Video timelines benefit from more memory — the jump from 16GB to 32GB is more useful than the jump from a good GPU to a marginally better one, in most editing workflows.

### Gaming

Three numbers decide whether you'll be happy: the refresh rate of the panel, the power limit of the GPU, and whether the machine can sustain both without throttling into a jet engine. TUF gets you into RTX territory for less; ROG buys you a better screen, better cooling and higher power limits. If you mostly play older titles or esports, the cheaper TUF configurations are genuinely enough and the extra spend is better used on a good external monitor.

### If you mostly browse, stream and write

Chromebooks and the cheapest Vivobooks do this fine. Paying for a high-refresh gaming panel to read email is a donation to the manufacturer.
